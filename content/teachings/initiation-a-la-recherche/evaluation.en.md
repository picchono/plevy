## Submitting the paper

### Choosing the right venue

Submitting a paper begins with choosing the venue (conference or journal). This choice depends on several factors:
- **The nature of the results**: Preliminary or exploratory results are better suited to conferences; consolidated and in-depth results to journals.
- **Timing**: Conferences have fixed deadlines (call for papers); journals accept submissions on a rolling basis.
- **Target audience**: Each venue has its own community, expectations, and format (pictorial, traditional article, research note).
- **Prestige and scope**: Consult rankings (CORE for computer science conferences, Scimago for journals) to assess scope.

In design research, the main conferences are DIS, CHI, DRS, E&PDE, IASDR. The main journals are *Design Studies*, *Design Issues*, *She Ji*, *International Journal of Design*, *CoDesign*, *Sciences du Design*.

### Preparing the submission

Before submitting:
- **Check the author guidelines**: Format, number of pages, anonymity (double-blind review), templates.
- **Ensure the paper is complete**: Clear introduction, detailed method, precise results, open discussion.
- **Have it reviewed by peers**: Colleagues, co-authors, or lab members before submission.
- **Write a submission letter** (if required): Briefly present the paper and its relevance to the venue.

### Understanding the process

Most venues use peer review:
1. The submitter sends the paper.
2. The editor or chair assigns 2 to 4 reviewers.
3. The reviewers evaluate the paper against predefined criteria.
4. The editor makes a decision: accept, minor revisions, major revisions, reject.

The process typically takes 2 to 6 months for conferences, 3 to 12 months for journals.

## Peer review

### What reviewers evaluate

Reviewers generally assess:
- **Relevance**: Does the paper match the scope of the venue?
- **Originality**: Does it offer a new contribution?
- **Rigor**: Is the method sound? Are the results well supported?
- **Clarity**: Is the writing understandable? Are the figures and tables clear?
- **Significance**: Is the contribution meaningful to the community?

### Types of decisions

- **Accept**: Rare on the first submission. The paper is accepted as-is or with minor corrections.
- **Minor revisions**: Formatting corrections, clarifications, additional references. Generally accepted if handled well.
- **Major revisions**: Substantial changes required (new analysis, rewording, additional experiments). Possible but demanding.
- **Reject**: The paper is not accepted. It may be submitted elsewhere after revision.

### Handling feedback

Receiving criticism can be difficult, especially for early-career researchers. Some advice:
- **Wait before reacting**: Read the reviews, then take some time (a few days) before starting to respond.
- **Distinguish substance from form**: Some critiques are misunderstandings (to be clarified), others are valid (to be addressed).
- **Don't take it personally**: The critiques are about the work, not about you.
- **Identify common themes**: If multiple reviewers raise the same issue, it is likely something that needs to be addressed.

## Revisions

### Responding to reviewers

The response letter is a crucial document. It should:
- **Thank the reviewers** for their time and comments.
- **Respond point by point** to each comment, quoting the comment then the response.
- **Indicate the changes** made (or explain why a change was not made).
- **Be respectful and professional**, even if the critiques are harsh.

Example structure:
```
Reviewer 1, comment 1: "The method lacks details on participant recruitment."
Response: "We have added a Section 3.2 detailing the recruitment procedure, pp. 7-8."
```

### Making the revisions

- **Follow a logical order**: Start with major changes (structure, analyses), then minor ones (text, figures).
- **Mark the changes**: Use track changes mode or highlight the modified passages.
- **Check consistency**: Ensure the changes do not create inconsistencies in the rest of the text.

### Resubmitting

Once the revisions are complete:
- Submit the new version of the paper.
- Submit the response to reviewers.
- Submit a marked-up version of the changes (if requested).

The editor will review the changes and make a new decision. If the revisions are satisfactory, the paper is accepted. Otherwise, further revisions may be requested, or the paper may be rejected.

## Practical advice

- **Start early**: Do not wait until the last minute to submit. Allow time for revisions.
- **Learn from the process**: Every submission, even if rejected, is an opportunity to improve your work and your understanding of research.
- **Build a network**: Attend conferences, discuss with other researchers, and get informal feedback before submitting.
- **Persevere**: Rejection is part of the process. Most accepted papers have been rejected at least once before.
